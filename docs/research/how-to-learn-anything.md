# How to Learn (Almost) Anything in 48 Hours — Tansel Ali (Hardie Grant, 2015)

**Mined through the lens:** practical skill acquisition — deconstruction into sub-skills, minimum
effective dose, 80/20 targeting, deliberate-practice structure, curriculum sequencing for fastest
visible competence.

**Source file:** `corpus/how-to-learn-anything.txt` (1,253 lines). Line numbers below are anchors
into that file so any claim can be re-verified.

---

## 0. What this book actually is (read this before trusting any of it)

Tansel Ali is a 3× Australian Memory Champion. This is a **mnemonics** book, not a skill-acquisition
book. Its center of gravity is: convert abstract information into vivid images, link the images into
a story, review on a schedule. Everything else — languages, music, study, goal-setting — is that one
engine pointed at a domain.

**What that means for us.** It is *extremely* strong on the thing we are weakest at (how do you get
a tired, embarrassed A1 learner to actually retain 300 job phrases at 5 min/day?) and *nearly silent*
on things a naive reader will assume it covers:

- **No Ericsson-style deliberate practice.** No feedback-loop design, no error-taxonomy, no
  "practice at the edge of ability" mechanic. The closest it gets is "go use it, note what didn't
  work, re-memorise" (l.26–28).
- **No real 80/20 / frequency analysis.** It asserts "1500 phrases" (l.845) with zero corpus
  reasoning. There is no frequency list, no coverage math, no argument for *which* 1500.
- **No curriculum sequencing** beyond one three-step order: pronunciation → phrases → review (l.513).
  That order, though, is load-bearing and correct (see §3).
- **Evidence is anecdotal.** One man's Yellow Pages feat, competition results, and a Mandarin
  self-experiment. Zero citations to memory science. The spaced-repetition ladder (l.25) is folk
  wisdom, not FSRS/SM-2.
- **Mnemonics produce recall of a *form*, not fluent production under pressure.** The book half-admits
  this: *"Memorisation only helps you to store the information, whereas learning helps you understand.
  Practise is the intersection where these two meet"* (l.28). It does not tell you how to get from
  recall to automaticity. **This is the biggest gap and we must not build a mnemonic app and call it
  a learning system.**

**Extraction gaps to flag for synthesis:** the text jumps from Chapter 2 (l.46) straight to Chapter 4
(l.121) — **Chapter 3 "Memory Techniques" (Method of Loci, mind mapping, speed reading) is missing
from this extract**, even though it is referenced repeatedly (l.81, l.115, l.139, l.213, l.272, l.327).
**Chapter 8 "Speaking to an Audience"** is also absent from the body (only in the TOC at l.7 and the
sources at l.1204). Chapter 10 (Music) is mislabeled "CHAPTER 7" at l.881. If synthesis needs Method
of Loci in depth, get it from another source.

**Net verdict:** treat this as our *retention and encoding* source. Take the phrase-unit thesis, the
encoding-beats-repetition thesis, the pronunciation-first ordering, the chunk-escalation progression,
and the accountability/planning discipline. Get corpus selection, feedback design, and automaticity
from elsewhere (Fluent Forever, Design for How People Learn, Accelerated Learning).

## 1. The 7-step meta-process, and what its time budget reveals

Front matter, l.17–28. The whole book is scaffolded on this:

| # | Step | Budget | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gather materials/resources | ≤3 h | books, audio, apps, **and a native speaker to practise with** |
| 2 | Develop memorisation strategy | ≤2 h | pick the technique *before* you start, based on the shape of the material |
| 3 | Organise/prioritise materials | ≤1 h | get the corpus into a form you can grind linearly (he says: a spreadsheet) |
| 4 | **Create accountability** | ≤1 h | tell a person. *"We do tend to slack off if we are accountable to only ourselves."* |
| 5 | Memorise | ≤30 h | short blocks, not long ones |
| 6 | Review (spaced repetition) | ≤1 h | 1h → 1d → 1w → 1mo → 3mo → 6mo → 1y |
| 7 | Practise and apply | ≤10 h | real environment, log failures, **go back and re-memorise** |

**Three non-obvious readings of this table:**

1. **83% of the budget is steps 5+7 (memorise + real-world use).** Only ~12% is planning. But the
   planning is fully front-loaded and *non-optional* — you do not touch the material until you have
   chosen a technique and named an accountability partner.
2. **Accountability gets its own budgeted hour, before any learning happens.** It is treated as
   infrastructure, not motivation garnish.
3. **Step 7 loops back into step 5.** *"Note all these down and figure out why these were the case.
   Learn from them and then go back and re-memorise"* (l.28). Real-world failure is a **content
   routing signal**, not just encouragement.

> **Applied.** Our onboarding is currently role + placement exam. It should be a compressed
> version of steps 1–4: (a) name the goal as a *felt scene*, (b) name the barriers, (c) show the
> arithmetic, (d) **name a human accountability partner** (supervisor or shift-mate) — before the
> first drill. That is four screens and it is worth more than any feature in the backlog.

## 2. THE headline finding: phrases, not words

Chapter 9, l.505–508. Quoted almost in full because this is the single most valuable page in the book
for us:

> *"Many people believe we need to learn and memorise vocabulary to learn and speak a language. While
> this may be true in one sense, memorising hundreds or even thousands of words doesn't mean that
> you'll learn the language. This is because words on their own have no context and meanings can
> differ enormously depending on the situation."* (l.505)

> *"We learned our mother tongue through repeated phrases, so it makes sense for us to memorise
> phrases instead of individual words... Memorising phrases helps us learn things in context and gets
> us speaking much faster than if learning individual words. The concept then is: the more phrases
> you memorise, the more language you will be able to speak."* (l.507–508)

And the dosing math (l.845–848):

- *"Learning 1500 phrases will have you speaking the language at its very basic form."*
- *"If you spend around forty minutes a day memorising twenty-five phrases then you'll pick up the
  spoken language in two months. That is less than forty-eight hours' study!"*
- *"If you like, you can build on this with seventeen phrases a day for three months."*

The worked examples confirm the unit is a whole utterance, not a word: the Mandarin list (l.851–862)
and French list (l.864–869) are entirely functional sentences — *"Can you speak slowly?", "Give me a
hand.", "How much?", "Could you write it down for me, please?", "Do you take credit cards?"*

**Do the arithmetic for us.** Tansel's rate is 40 min ÷ 25 phrases ≈ **1.6 min per phrase, encoded**.
At our 5 min/day target that is ~3 new phrases/day. Over 90 days: **~270 phrases.**

Now the crucial reframe: Tansel needs 1500 because he is trying to cover *a whole general language*.
We are not. A bellboy's guest-facing English is a closed set. Greeting, luggage, room escort, amenity
explanation, directions, taxi, weather, apology, handoff. Realistically **250–400 utterances covers
~95% of what a botones ever needs to say on shift.**

> **This is the strategic core of the product and it is arithmetically defensible:**
> *"Duolingo is trying to teach you a language. We are trying to teach you a job. Your job's English
> is 300 sentences. Three sentences a day. You're done in three months."*
>
> Our 80/20 is not "learn less English" — it is **"the domain is small; we scoped it, so you don't
> have to."** That is a claim Duolingo structurally cannot make and an HR director can verify.

> **Applied — concretely:**
> - **Audit `src/content/exam.ts` and the drills DB for any word-level items and kill them.** No
>   isolated-vocabulary cards. If "luggage" needs learning, it is learned inside *"May I take your
>   luggage, sir?"*
> - **Define the canonical corpus per role as a numbered, finite, published list.** `botones-300.ts`,
>   `recepcion-320.ts`, `restaurante-280.ts`. Publish the count to the learner and to the buyer.
>   Finiteness is the product.
> - **Source it from real guest speech, not a textbook.** The book's own sourcing advice is
>   wikitravel phrasebooks (l.1218) — the analogue for us is: sit in a partner hotel lobby for a week
>   and transcribe what guests actually say and what staff actually fail to say. That is step 1
>   ("gather materials, ≤3 hours") done properly, and it doubles as customer development.
> - **Every corpus item is a full utterance with a role, a trigger, and a register.** Schema:
>   `{ id, role, cefr, guest_trigger, staff_utterance, es_gloss, ipa_hint, mnemonic_es, audio_url, chunk_size }`.

**Loud conflict with the naive default:** every language app on the market — and every content team
you'd hire — will want to build vocabulary decks, because words are cheap to author, easy to test,
and easy to count for a progress bar. The book says words are the *wrong unit* because they carry no
context. Authoring 300 excellent situated utterances is harder and slower than authoring 3,000
words, and it is the correct trade.

## 3. Pronunciation is step 1, and skipping it poisons everything downstream

Chapter 9, l.513–515. The explicit order:

> *"1. learn and memorise the pronunciations 2. memorise the phrases 3. review phrases."*
>
> *"If you don't learn the correct pronunciation then the memory connections you make will be
> incorrect. Yes, you'll likely remember them, but you'll end up saying the wrong thing! It is
> essential to spend time memorising the pronunciations."* (l.515)

And the closing checklist repeats it first: *"Make sure you memorise the pronunciations before
memorising phrases. It will make learning much more effective."* (l.871–872)

**Look at the *format* he uses.** The Pinyin table (l.523–611) and the French table (l.836) are
**contrastive maps anchored to words the learner already knows**:

- *"b — Similar to 'b' in 'boat', softened to a 'p' sound."*
- *"q — Similar to 'ch' in 'cheap': tongue is positioned below lower teeth."*
- *"eu — Similar to 'e' in 'me', but faster."*
- *"an — Nasal sound; like 'ang' in 'gang', without the 'g'."*

Every entry: target sound → nearest sound in a word they already own → the delta. Plus articulator
instruction where the delta is large ("tongue below lower teeth"). This is ~40 rows and it comes
before a single phrase.

> **Applied — build a "Mapa de sonidos" module and put it in front of everything.**
> Spanish→English has a small, *enumerable* set of failure modes. Ship 15–18 cards, in Spanish,
> each anchored to a Spanish word:
>
> | English sound | Anchor / delta (in Spanish) | Job-critical minimal pair |
> |---|---|---|
> | /ɪ/ vs /iː/ | "*ship* no es *sheep*. La i corta es más relajada, como la i de *misa* dicha rápido." | *sit / seat*, *ship / sheep* |
> | /b/ vs /v/ | "En inglés la *v* toca el labio con los dientes. En español no." | *very / berry* |
> | /s/ inicial | "*Speak* empieza en S. No digas *espeak*." | *stay, spa, street* |
> | /θ/ | "Lengua entre los dientes. Como la *z* de España." | *bath, three, thank* |
> | /h/ | "Se pronuncia, suave. No es la *j* de *jamón*." | *hotel, help, here* |
> | /d/ vs /ð/ | "*the* es suave, con la lengua entre dientes." | *the, they, there* |
> | final consonants | "En inglés la última letra se dice. *Bag*, no *ba*." | *bags, rooms, guests* |
> | /r/ | "No la ruedes. La lengua no toca el paladar." | *right away, room, restaurant* |
>
> **Why this is urgent and not cosmetic:** our `/api/score-speaking` path runs Whisper. If a learner
> says *"I espeak"* and Whisper mis-transcribes, we currently tell them they're wrong **without
> telling them why**. For a learner who already believes they're bad at English, an unexplained
> failure is a churn event. The sound map gives us a *diagnosis vocabulary*: "Tu problema fue la S
> inicial — mira la tarjeta 3." That turns a demoralising score into a specific, fixable, 20-second
> lesson.
>
> Rubric change in `.orcha/phase-3-ai-scoring.md`: have the scorer return a **`pronunciation_fault`
> enum** drawn from the sound-map card IDs, not just a numeric score. Then route the learner to that
> card.

**Loud conflict with the naive default:** every product team ships phonetics late because it feels
"advanced" and it's unglamorous to author. The book says it is *first*, and the reason is
asymmetric cost — a phrase learned with a wrong sound is worse than a phrase not learned, because
you now have to unlearn it, and the learner will use it confidently in front of a guest and fail.

## 4. Encoding quality replaces repetition volume — 3–4 reps, not 100

Chapter 9, l.508:

> *"You may repeat phrases hundreds of times, while memory techniques may only require three or four
> repetitions. Memory techniques enable you to learn much faster, with better long-term recognition."*

Chapter 7, l.395: *"Repetition sucks. It takes a long time and we often forget and are forced to
repeat things over and over again. Today's students are given so much information that they don't
have time to review material so it's no surprise they end up forgetting it."*

Chapter 2, l.50: *"Words are processed on one side of the brain, images on the other. Repeating words
is ineffective, but creating images from those words is incredibly strong."*

**This is the feasibility argument for the entire product.** Run the numbers:

- Repetition-based learning (Duolingo model): ~50–100 exposures per item to reach durable recall.
  300 items × 75 reps × ~4 s = **~25 hours of drilling**. At 5 min/day that is **300 days.** We
  cannot ship that promise.
- Encoding-based learning (this book): 3–4 exposures. 300 items × 4 reps × ~15 s (slower per rep,
  because encoding is richer) = **~5 hours.** At 5 min/day = **60 days**, plus the spaced review
  ladder. **That fits.**

> **Applied — this should redirect the content budget.** A naive roadmap spends the next quarter
> building 12 exercise *types* (match, type-what-you-hear, order-the-words, speak-it, fill-blank…)
> because variety feels like product. **Wrong axis.** The book says variety of drill does nothing;
> quality of *encoding* does everything.
>
> **Build one or two drill types and 300 excellent mnemonics.** Concretely: freeze the drill-type
> backlog; spend the same engineering/content effort on a `mnemonic_es` field for every corpus item
> and a human review pass on all of them. This is the highest-ROI content decision available to us.
>
> Also: **stop measuring "exposures" and start measuring "successful unaided recalls."** If our
> analytics reward volume, we will optimise toward the repetition model by accident.

## 5. The mnemonic engine, and the fact that we must ship it pre-built

### 5a. Yellow Elephant Memory Model (Ch2, l.96–108)

**Abstract → Image → Association → Communication.**

1. **Abstract** — anything without physical form: a foreign phrase, a name, a number. *"Abstract things
   are slippery to understand and don't mean anything to us unless an image is created inside our
   brain."* (l.100)
2. **Image** — convert it to something seeable.
3. **Association** — link the images into a story. Critical detail: **the connection must be
   physical.** *"You may even imagine Clare being chased by a bear — but because there is no physical
   connection or contact between Clare and the bear it weakens the memorisation."* (l.111) Pen *next
   to* paper is weak; pen *shredding* paper is strong (l.64, l.104).
4. **Communication** — *"What we create for ourselves may not suit or be appropriate for our audience
   so we need to consider new ways to craft information that others can understand."* (l.106–108)

**Step 4 is the product step and almost every reader skips it.** It is the author explicitly saying:
a mnemonic built for yourself does not transfer; if you are delivering to an audience you must
re-craft it for them.

### 5b. SMASHIN SCOPE (Ch2, l.56–95)

Twelve levers to make an image stick: **S**enses/synaesthesia, **M**ovement, **A**ssociation,
**S**exuality/Self, **H**umour, **I**magination, **N**umbers, **S**ymbolism, **C**olour, **O**rder,
**P**ositive images, **E**xaggeration.

The operative instruction is *anti-logic*: *"Try not to use your everyday logic when creating stories"*
(l.91). *"I sat down on the chair; I stopped at the red light; I typed on my laptop. These examples
are perfectly normal, but they are not memorable... how about the chair turning around, jumping and
then sitting on you?"* (l.71). Also note **negative/disgusting imagery is explicitly endorsed**:
*"The brain loves drama and gets attached to it"* (l.83).

Cost annotation: *"around thirty seconds to create a story using SMASHIN SCOPE. For difficult,
non-concrete words, it may take up to one minute."* (l.95)

### 5c. The language-phrase template (Ch9, l.840–841, l.616ff)

Two worked examples, and they share a precise structure:

> **Nǐ hǎo ma?** ("How are you?")
> *Image:* Nǐ (**knee**) hǎo (**how**) ma (**ma**, as in mother).
> *Association:* "You hit your **knee** on the table and started to scream in pain. You hear a voice
> behind you asking: '**Howww** did you do that?' You turn around and see that it was your **ma**,
> who then says, **'How are you?'**"

> **Comment allez-vous?** ("How are you?")
> *Image:* Comment (**comment**) allez (**Ali**) vous (**you**).
> *Association:* "A really funny **comment** was made to **Ali**. He turned around and asked **'How
> are you?'** to the person making the remarks."

**Decompose the template:**
1. Break the target utterance into 2–4 **sound-alike fragments in the learner's L1 or in words they
   already own.**
2. Build one absurd micro-scene physically linking those fragments.
3. **End the scene by having a character speak the meaning out loud.** Both examples do this. The
   story is a *bridge from sound to meaning*, and the meaning is the punchline.

Permission to be sloppy: *"Don't worry about getting the exact memory associations for words or
phrases. Even making an association to the first letter can be enough to trigger the rest of the
word."* (l.879) — i.e. the hook only needs to be a **retrieval cue**, not a full phonetic encoding.
That massively lowers our authoring bar.

### 5d. Applied: this is a content-production spec

> Our learner is described in our own brief as *"lazy about effort, resistant to change, low
> tolerance for friction."* They will **not** author their own SMASHIN SCOPE stories. So we invoke
> step 4 (Communication) and **ship the mnemonic**, in Spanish, pre-built, human-reviewed.
>
> **Worked examples in our domain** (this is the format, three per role, then scale with Claude +
> human review):
>
> - **"Right away, sir."** → fragments *rait / a-wéi*. → *"Un **REY** llega tarde y grita **'¡A
>   HUEVO!'**… tú corres y le dices: **Right away, sir** — enseguida, señor."* Hook: REY → "right".
> - **"May I take your bags?"** → *mei / teik / bags*. → *"En **MAYO** un huésped trae **BOLSAS** tan
>   grandes que se **TAMBALEA**. Tú las agarras: **May I take your bags?**"*
> - **"Enjoy your stay."** → *in-yói / stéi*. → *"El huésped **ESTÁ** feliz porque le diste una
>   **JOYA**. **Enjoy your stay** — que disfrute su estancia."* (bonus: *stay ≈ está* is a real
>   semantic bridge, not just phonetic.)
> - **"Housekeeping."** → *haus / kii / ping*. → *"Tocas la puerta de la **CASA**, tienes la **QUE**
>   (llave) en la mano, y adentro alguien juega **PING**-pong. **Housekeeping.**"*
> - **"How may I help you?"** → *jau / mei / jelp*. → *"Un huésped se cae y grita. Le tiendes la mano:
>   **How may I help you?**"* (physical connection: you are *touching* him.)
>
> **Rules for the mnemonic field, enforced in the content editor (Master OS):**
> 1. Written in Spanish (Mexico).
> 2. 2–4 sound fragments, each anchored to a word a Mexican adult already knows.
> 3. The elements must **physically touch/act on each other** (l.111 — chased-by-bear is too weak).
> 4. The scene **must end with the English utterance and its Spanish meaning spoken**.
> 5. Absurd, exaggerated, or gross is fine and preferred (l.83, l.85). **Never** infantilising —
>   this collides with Design System principle #1 (*respeto, no condescendencia*), so the register
>   must be "chiste de cocina entre adultos", not "cuento infantil". Worth a written copy standard.
> 6. ≤ 25 words. It has to be readable in the 15 seconds the drill budgets.
>
> **The generation-effort tension, resolved cheaply.** The book is clear that *self*-generated images
> are stronger. But generation costs us 30–60 s/item (l.95), which is 10–20% of the daily 5-minute
> budget for one item. Compromise: ship the hook, then ask **one trivial question about the hook**
> ("¿Qué traía el huésped en la mano?"). That converts passive reading into an act of processing at
> ~3 seconds' cost. Do not make them author.

## 6. Spaced review: the exact ladder, and the 1-hour touch nobody builds

Step 6, l.25:

> *"the rule for spaced repetition is to review an hour later, then a day later, then a week later, a
> month later, three months later, six months later and finally a year later."*

Seven touches: **1h → 1d → 1w → 1mo → 3mo → 6mo → 1y.** Fixed ladder, not adaptive. Crude next to
FSRS, but note what it gets right that most implementations get wrong: **the same-day, one-hour
review.**

Corroborating practice from the training program (Ch12): *"Memorise six decimal digits per location
for twenty minutes, then go back and review for ten minutes"* (l.1142–1143), and the explicit warning
*"don't memorise for one hour straight, you'll become tired and may end up forgetting everything"*
(l.1145). Also the random-words protocol: memorise → recall → **review your mistakes, visualise them**
→ memorise again → repeat until clean (l.1165).

> **Applied — the 1-hour ping is a WhatsApp-native feature the PWA cannot do, and Duolingo does not do.**
>
> - **10:00** — daily drill (3 new phrases, ~4 min).
> - **11:00** — WhatsApp message: 3 items, unaided recall. *"¿Cómo dices 'enseguida, señor'?"*
>   Reply by voice note or text. ~40 seconds.
> - **Next day** — those 3 items appear as the first items of the new drill.
> - Then 1w / 1mo / 3mo lanes.
>
> This makes the WhatsApp channel *pedagogically necessary* rather than a convenience wrapper — it
> is the only channel that can interrupt at T+1h. That is a real moat and a real answer to "why not
> just build an app."
>
> - **Implementation:** the review ladder needs a `review_queue` table keyed
>   `(learner_id, corpus_item_id, due_at, stage)` with stages `h1,d1,w1,m1,m3,m6,y1`. Ship the fixed
>   ladder first — it's a day of work and 80% of the benefit. Adaptive scheduling is a later
>   optimisation, not a v1 requirement.
> - **Mistake handling per l.1165:** a miss does not just reschedule — it **re-shows the mnemonic**
>   ("visualise them") before re-queueing. Our current model probably just decrements a score.
>   Failure must route back to *encoding*, not to *more reps*.

## 7. Chunk-size escalation: the correct progression axis

Chapter 12, l.1111–1124. This is the most transferable *sequencing* idea in the book.

Memorising 40 digits, three strategies:

- **2 digits per location** → 20 short stories per row. *"there is a high possibility of making one
  mistake, or even two, out of that twenty."* (l.1116)
- **4 digits per location** → 10 stories. *"It's a little longer but now there is more of a storyline
  instead of very short connections... **A storyline is always more effective than a simple link
  because we can relate to it, because it has meaning.** It is much more difficult to create meaning
  or a storyline for one particular image."* (l.1120)
- **6 or 10 digits per location** → 4–7 stories. *"if you only have four stories to remember per row
  of forty digits, chances are you will remember them."* (l.1121)

Also l.1147: *"It makes sense to use more digits in a location for one-hour numbers so you have fewer
stories and locations to remember."* And the summary bullet: *"Adding more information to be
remembered into one location makes it more memorable."* (l.1177)

**Two mechanisms, and both matter:**
1. **Fewer retrieval events = fewer failure points.** Error probability compounds across chunks.
2. **Bigger chunks carry narrative meaning; small chunks are arbitrary links.** Meaning is free
   glue.

> **Applied — this is our CEFR progression spec, and it inverts the industry default.**
>
> The naive move as a learner levels up: *more items per session.* Wrong. The book says: **hold the
> item count constant, grow the chunk.**
>
> | Level | Chunk = one drill item | Items/session | Session length |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | A1 | one utterance ("Good morning, sir.") | 3 | ~3 min |
> | A2 | a two-turn exchange (guest asks → you answer) | 3 | ~4 min |
> | B1 | a full 4-turn interaction (greet → offer → confirm → close) | 3 | ~5 min |
> | B2 | a 4-turn interaction **with a complication injected** (guest is angry / doesn't understand / changes their mind mid-way) | 2–3 | ~6 min |
>
> **Session length grows ~2×; item count is flat; chunk size grows 4–6×.** The learner experiences
> "I'm handling a whole guest now," which is a far better felt-progress signal than "I did 20
> exercises instead of 10."
>
> Add `chunk_size` (1 = utterance, 2 = exchange, 4 = interaction, 4+ = interaction-with-complication)
> to the corpus schema, and make CEFR level a *function of chunk size*, not of vocabulary difficulty.
> This also gives the HR dashboard a much better story: "Rosa went from single phrases to handling a
> full check-in conversation" beats "Rosa is at A2."

## 8. Match the technique to the retrieval context (the "egg on your face" principle)

Two stories, back to back, and together they are the sharpest strategic lesson in the book.

**The radio failure (l.273):** Tansel — a national memory champion who routinely memorises hundreds of
words — was given a 10-word test live on air. He used the Method of Loci, his best technique.
**He got 3 out of 10.** Listeners called in saying he was a fake.

> *"From this I learned that you might know sensational memory techniques but if you don't use the
> right one for the right occasion you'll end up with egg on your face."*

**The Yellow Pages decision (l.272):** he then *deliberately rejected* the Method of Loci — the
technique he calls "one of the most powerful memory techniques there is" — for the 2,000-ad job:

> *"Firstly, it would have taken longer... Secondly, I didn't have enough locations. Thirdly, **as I
> was going to be tested at random, there was no point trying to remember the order**, which is the
> Method of Loci's specialty."*

He picked basic linking instead, because **the retrieval context was random-access, not sequential.**

> **Applied — describe our actual retrieval context, then build backwards from it.**
>
> The learner's real test is: **standing in front of a guest, in noise, under time pressure, being
> interrupted, phone in pocket, cued by something the *guest* said, in random order, with an
> emotional cost to failing.**
>
> Now audit our drills against that:
>
> | Retrieval context feature | What we must train | What we probably do today |
> |---|---|---|
> | Cued by the *guest's* utterance | Prompt is **guest audio in an accent**, answer is the staff line | Prompt is Spanish text → produce English |
> | Random order | Never drill in corpus order; shuffle within role always | Likely ordered lessons |
> | Time pressure | **Response window (~4 s)**; a slow-but-correct answer is a partial fail | Untimed |
> | Noise | Some items with lobby/restaurant background audio | Clean studio audio |
> | Interruption | Guest talks over you / changes request mid-turn (B1+) | Not modelled |
> | Emotional cost | Practice must be **private and unjudged**; the *shift* is where it counts | Scores shown immediately |
>
> **The single most important change here: flip the drill prompt from Spanish→English to
> guest-audio→response.** Spanish→English trains translation. The job requires *reacting to English
> speech*. Those are different retrieval paths and we are currently training the wrong one. This is
> the "egg on your face" failure mode, exactly.
>
> **Second most important: add a response timer.** Guests do not wait. An answer produced in 9
> seconds is not a usable answer. Score `correct AND under 4s` as the target state; surface
> "correcto pero lento" as its own feedback state.

## 9. Practise = the real environment, and the failure log routes back into content

Step 7, l.26–28:

> *"If you have indeed memorised 1500 French phrases, go into an environment where French is spoken
> and have conversations. Are you able to speak it? **What works? What doesn't? Note all these down
> and figure out why these were the case. Learn from them and then go back and re-memorise.**"*

And l.878: *"Make sure you can practise with someone who speaks the language. **You don't want to be
memorising for hours only to have done it incorrectly!**"*

Note also that Tansel's own quality control during the Yellow Pages run was **his wife testing him
every night** (l.274) — a free human in the room, not a tool.

> **Applied — two features, and one of them also closes the enterprise sale.**
>
> **(a) Misión en turno.** Once a week, WhatsApp assigns one phrase as a real-world mission: *"Hoy,
> con un huésped de verdad, di: 'May I take your bags, sir?' Cuando lo hagas, contesta aquí:
> ✅ salió bien / 😬 no me entendió / 😶 no me atreví."*
> - `✅` → advance the item, and **log the win** (this is the emotional payoff the whole product is
>   for).
> - `😬` → route to the **pronunciation sound-map card** for that phrase's known fault, then requeue.
> - `😶` → this is not a language problem, it's a confidence problem. Different intervention:
>   lower-stakes version of the same phrase, or a "practice it with a compañero first" prompt.
>
> That three-way branch is the book's "note what didn't work and figure out **why**" made mechanical.
> **We currently have no signal at all from the actual job.** This is the only data that proves the
> product works, and it is a WhatsApp text reply — trivial to collect.
>
> **(b) The supervisor is the free native-speaker tester.** Our buyer wants "near-zero implementation
> work." Give the supervisor a **60-second weekly card** (WhatsApp or a printable slip from the HR
> dashboard): *"Esta semana, pídele a Rosa que diga estas 3 frases. Marca ✅ o ❌."* Three taps.
>
> This does four things at once: it is the book's step-7 human test; it is the book's step-4
> accountability; it is real evidence for the HR dashboard that beats app-usage metrics; and it makes
> the supervisor a stakeholder in the program's success, which is the single best defence against
> churn at renewal.

## 10. Accountability is to a *person* — the book budgets an hour for it before you start

Step 4, l.23: *"It is important to share your learning task with a family member, friend, or anyone
else that will hold you accountable. Accountability to others creates motivation to get you going so
that you don't let others down. **We do tend to slack off if we are accountable to only ourselves.**"*

Ch5, l.232–233: *"Being accountable for your actions increases the chances of reaching your goals.
It's easier, too, if there are other people, friends or family, who share the same goal... **You can
let yourself down, but letting others down is much more disappointing.**"*

Yellow Pages: *"What helped me get through this challenge was the fact that I **tweeted my progress
daily**, which held me accountable to my followers and friends"* (l.279).

Ch7, l.483: *"Keep at it and get others involved. **Social learning makes you learn even faster.**"*

> **Applied — this is the direct, named attack on Duolingo's streak.**
>
> A streak is accountability *to yourself* — precisely the kind the book says fails. Replace/augment:
>
> 1. **Onboarding asks for a name.** *"¿Quién va a saber que estás haciendo esto?"* Pick a
>    compañero de turno or your supervisor from a list. This is the book's step 4 and it costs one
>    screen.
> 2. **Cohort board by shift, not by property.** Your five shift-mates' progress, visible. Small
>    group, real faces, people you see tonight. (We already have cohort infrastructure — point it at
>    shift teams.)
> 3. **The supervisor weekly card (§9b)** doubles as the accountability touch.
> 4. **Public-ish progress.** The Yellow Pages analogue: a printed board in the staff room updated
>    weekly from the HR dashboard. Low-tech, zero-friction, and works for the learner who won't open
>    an app.
>
> **Do keep a streak** — it's cheap and it does something. But do not let it be the *primary*
> commitment device. The book is unambiguous that solitary accountability is the weak version.

## 11. Plan then act: barriers first, arithmetic visible, session length earned

### 11a. Identify the barriers *before* the plan (Ch5, l.244–245)

> *"Make a list of the things that are stopping you from learning as you'd like to. You may find that
> the only barriers are ones you've created in your mind. Or they could be physical, financial,
> time-based, skills-based or geographical."*

The full failure taxonomy the chapter opens with (l.223–241): lack of discipline, lack of passion,
lack of focus (*"Your goals end up being 'too hard'... you start to feel overwhelmed and you give
up"*), lack of accountability, too busy, too forgetful, negative thoughts.

> **Applied — onboarding must ask what's blocking you and then *reconfigure the product*, not just
> record the answer.** Five checkboxes, in Spanish, each wired to a real config change:
>
> | Barrier | Product response |
> |---|---|
> | "Casi no tengo datos" | WhatsApp text-first mode; no audio autoplay; pre-download the week's audio on wifi |
> | "Mi celular está lleno" | Skip PWA install prompt entirely; WhatsApp-only path |
> | "Trabajo de noche" | Drill window 15:00–17:00, not 08:00 |
> | "Me da pena hablar inglés" | Private-practice mode: no scores shown for first 7 days; solo record-and-listen |
> | "Ya intenté y no pude" | Day-1 is the method demo (§11c) with guaranteed success |
>
> Naive onboarding asks role + level. The book says the *barriers* determine whether anything
> happens at all.

### 11b. Pre-commit the arithmetic (Ch5, l.266)

Before accepting the Yellow Pages job Tansel did the math: *"if it took thirty seconds to memorise
one advertisement, then I should be able to manage 2000 ads in around twenty days."* Every technique
in the book carries a **"How long?" annotation** — 30 s per story (l.95), 3 min for ten items (l.186,
l.197), 2 min for 20 digits (l.138).

> **Applied — two places:**
> 1. **Every drill shows its honest time cost before it starts.** "Ejercicio de hoy · 3 frases · 4
>    minutos." For a tired worker on a break, a *bounded* task has a fraction of the activation cost
>    of an unbounded "lesson."
> 2. **Show the learner the whole arithmetic on day 1, as a contract, not a progress bar.**
>    *"Tu trabajo son 300 frases. 3 frases al día. En 90 días las tienes todas."* A progress bar
>    shows where you are; the arithmetic shows that finishing is *possible*, which is what our
>    "low prior success with school-style English" learner actually doubts.
> 3. Same arithmetic, re-pointed, is the HR pitch: *"90 días × 5 min = 7.5 horas por empleado. Cero
>    horas de tu tiempo."*

### 11c. Start short, grow — and day 1 is not an assessment (l.24, l.274–276)

> *"It is best to start with short periods of memorisation rather than long... it is less strain on
> the brain, you will complete a set memorisation period quickly, and as you get better you will
> increase your time. **If you start with longer memorisation periods then it will overwhelm you
> very quickly.**"* (l.24)

And the night-one story, which is the most human passage in the book (l.274–276, l.280):

- **Night 1:** 70 minutes → 50 ads. His wife tested him. *"the results were disappointing. I didn't
  know if I should continue, and I had barely even started."*
- **Night 2:** slightly better. **Night 3:** much better.
- Then steady state: 60 ads/hour, *"almost robotic."*
- **Second pass over the whole corpus: 120 ads/hour — double speed, better recall** (l.280).

**The method has a 2–3 session warm-up curve.** A national memory champion nearly quit after night one.

> **Applied — two hard product rules:**
> 1. **Session length is earned, not fixed.** Day 1–3 = **2 minutes, 1–2 phrases.** Grow to 5–7 min
>    over ~2 weeks, gated on completion not calendar. Shipping a uniform 5-minute session from day 1
>    to a learner who is tired and expects to fail is how you lose them in week one.
> 2. **Day 1 is a method tutorial with guaranteed success — not a diagnostic.** Our placement exam is
>    a *sales* instrument for the HR buyer (it produces the CEFR level they pay $50 for). It is a
>    terrible *first learner experience*: a 13-question diagnostic + listening + speaking test, for
>    someone whose whole history with English is failure, is a churn machine. **Decouple them.**
>    Learner's day 1 should be: one phrase, one mnemonic, one successful recall, done in 90 seconds,
>    ending in *"Ya sabes una frase más que ayer."* Run the placement exam on day 3–5, or frame it
>    explicitly as "esto no es un examen que se apruebe."
>
> This is a **concrete, checkable audit item** against the current `/exam/[id]/*` flow and the
> onboarding order in Phase 2.

### 11d. Make the goal a felt scene, not a metric (Ch5, l.229, l.252–253, l.283–285)

> *"Before embarking on a new project, create an image of your goal in your head. Write it down. Then
> hit it with all the feelings that achieving it will give you... The more emotion and feeling you
> have, the fiercer your pursuit of the goal will be."* (l.229)

The coaching formula (l.251–253) literally includes a **"How will it feel?"** column, and:
*"**If you don't have an emotional connection to a goal then you're only looking at a set of tasks
and to-do lists.**"* (l.285)

Language-chapter version: *"Visualise the end goal of speaking the language. How will it feel to
communicate with someone in their language? Use this as motivation to keep you going."* (l.875)

> **Applied.** "Llegar a B1" is a task list. Replace it, in onboarding, with one free-text or
> multiple-choice question in the learner's own frame:
>
> *"¿Qué quieres poder hacer en tu trabajo que hoy no puedes?"*
> — "Que un huésped me pida a mí" · "Entender cuando me hablan rápido" · "Que me pasen a recepción"
> · "Que no me dé pena" · [otra]
>
> Then **put that exact sentence at the top of the weekly progress screen and in the Monday WhatsApp
> message.** It costs one field and one string interpolation. The CEFR level is for the HR
> dashboard; the felt goal is for the learner. **They should never see the same primary metric.**

## 12. Batch-and-loop: the actual practice structure from the Yellow Pages run

Buried in Ch5 (l.276–280) is the most concrete practice protocol in the book, and it is *not* what a
modern SRS does:

> *"I was memorising sixty ads in sixty minutes... **I was memorising sixty ads and going back three
> more times to re-memorise them; I memorised sixty ads four times and then moved on to the next lot
> of sixty.**"*

Plus: 5 hours/day average; longest day 10 h as **five 2-hour blocks with breaks**; second full pass
over everything at double speed.

Corroborated by the training program (l.1142–1143, l.1161–1162): memorise 20 min → review 10 min →
repeat, for an hour. And random words (l.1165): memorise → recall → **review mistakes by visualising
them** → memorise again → repeat until clean.

**So the shape is: small batch → 4 passes on that batch → move on → later, a full second pass over
everything.** Not "one exposure then hand it to a global SRS queue."

> **Applied — restructure a session as batch-and-loop, not a flat queue:**
>
> ```
> DAILY DRILL (≈5 min)
>   Batch = today's 3 phrases.
>   Pass 1  see phrase + mnemonic + audio          (encode)
>   Pass 2  guest audio → recall (mnemonic shown on miss)
>   Pass 3  guest audio → recall (mnemonic hidden)
>   Pass 4  guest audio → speak it, timed <4s      (produce)
>   → then 2 items pulled from the spaced ladder (d1 / w1 / m1)
> T+1h WHATSAPP  3 unaided recalls from today's batch
> WEEKLY         full pass over the week's 15–20 phrases at speed
> ```
>
> Four passes on the same 3 items in one sitting looks redundant to a product manager and *is exactly
> what the book prescribes*. Note the passes are not identical repetitions — they escalate:
> recognise → recall-with-cue → recall-unaided → produce-under-time. That escalation is the closest
> thing to deliberate practice the book contains, and we should make it explicit.
>
> Also: the **weekly full pass** is missing from most SRS designs and is where his 2× speed gain came
> from (l.280). Cheap to build, big felt-fluency payoff.

## 13. Secondary observations worth carrying to synthesis

- **"I always assumed I had a bad memory"** (l.395). Tansel's own self-story before learning
  techniques is *identical* to our learner's ("soy malo para el inglés"). The reframe — *"no tienes
  mala memoria; te enseñaron con un método malo"* — is both an honest onboarding message and the best
  marketing line available to us, because the product actually delivers on it. Put it on the first
  screen the learner ever sees.
- **Anti-rote is the book's political position** (l.34, l.392–395): *"this book is a deliberate,
  conscious disruption to traditional learning methods, especially that of rote learning."* Our
  learners were failed by school-style English. Our product must *visibly not look like school* —
  which aligns exactly with Design System principle #2 (*editorial, no aplicación*) and #1
  (*respeto, no condescendencia*). Duolingo looks like a game; school looks like school; we should
  look like a professional tool.
- **Pomodoro, explicitly** (l.235): 25-min blocks. Not directly applicable at 5 min/day, but the
  underlying rule — *bounded block, visible end* — is.
- **Numbers are a special case with dedicated systems** (Major l.124–137, Dominic l.198–208). We
  have a real numbers problem: room numbers, times, prices, floors, phone numbers. *"Room 412",
  "checkout is at 12", "that'll be 850 pesos."* These are high-frequency and high-consequence, and
  they are the classic breakdown point for an L2 speaker under pressure. **Worth a dedicated
  "números en el turno" module** — English numbers 1–100 + times + currency, drilled to automaticity
  separately from phrases, because they combine freely rather than sitting inside fixed utterances.
  The book's systems are overkill for this, but its *insight* — that numbers need their own
  treatment because they're maximally abstract — is right and we are probably ignoring it.
- **Group by threes** (l.899, l.914–920): he consistently chunks into 3s/4s when making words from
  arbitrary sequences. Consistent with our 3-phrases-per-session dose.
- **Cost of the first mnemonic is higher than steady-state** (l.843): *"It will take some time at
  first to come up with associations for the foreign words, but a minute or two is all that's
  needed... The more you practice, the better you will get."* Another argument for the day-1-is-slow,
  session-length-is-earned rule.
- **The Communication step (l.106) is a licence for us to be the "memory coach."** The book's own
  model says the expert must re-craft their encoding for the audience. That is literally our job as a
  content company, and it justifies the content investment to Diego: we are not writing exercises,
  we are doing step 4 of the Yellow Elephant model at scale.

## 14. Where this book loudly contradicts what a naive product team would do

| # | Naive default | This book | Cost of getting it wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build vocabulary decks; count words learned | **Phrases only.** Words have no context (l.505) | You ship a worse Duolingo; learner "knows 500 words" and still can't greet a guest |
| 2 | Pronunciation is advanced; ship it later | **Pronunciation is step 1** (l.513–515) | Every phrase learned is encoded wrong; unlearning costs more than learning; Whisper scores them down with no explanation |
| 3 | More reps → more learning; build a big drill engine | **"Repetition sucks."** 3–4 reps with good encoding beats 100 (l.395, l.508) | The 5-min/day promise becomes arithmetically impossible |
| 4 | Ship 12 exercise types for variety | **One drill type + 300 excellent mnemonics.** Encoding quality is the lever | Quarter of engineering spent on the wrong axis |
| 5 | Level up = more exercises per session | **Level up = bigger chunks, same count** (l.1111–1124) | Sessions get longer and get abandoned; progress feels like grinding, not competence |
| 6 | Let users generate their own mnemonics (stronger!) | **Step 4: re-craft for your audience** (l.106). Ship it pre-built | Our "lazy, tired, low-friction-tolerance" learner generates nothing and drops out |
| 7 | Solitary streaks and gamified badges | **Accountability is to a named person** (l.23, l.233, l.279) | You inherit Duolingo's weakest retention mechanism and its ceiling |
| 8 | One "best" method, applied uniformly | **Match the method to the retrieval context** or get egg on your face (l.272–273) | We train Spanish→English translation; the job needs react-to-English-audio. Wrong skill entirely |
| 9 | "Practice" = more app time | **Practice = the real environment; log failures; route back to re-memorising** (l.26–28, l.878) | Zero evidence the product transfers to the job — which is exactly what the buyer is paying for |
| 10 | Fixed 5-min session from day 1 | **Start short, grow** (l.24) | Overwhelm in week one |
| 11 | Judge learner performance on day 1 (placement exam first) | **Night 1 was a disaster for a memory champion** (l.274) | Day-1 churn from the exact population that already expects to fail |
| 12 | Adaptive black-box scheduling; hide the plan | **Plan, then act.** Show the arithmetic (l.266); every task carries a stated time cost | Learner never believes finishing is possible |
| 13 | Onboard by asking role + level | **Ask what's blocking you, then reconfigure the product** (l.244) | Learner with no data plan gets an audio-heavy PWA and never opens it twice |
| 14 | Goal = "reach B1" | **Goal = a felt scene.** "Only a set of tasks and to-do lists" otherwise (l.285) | No emotional fuel; the metric that motivates the buyer demotivates the learner |

## 15. The one thing this book cannot give us

It gets a phrase into memory. It does not get it out of your mouth in under four seconds while a
guest is staring at you.

The book's only bridge is *"practise as much as you can"* (l.28) and *"build your muscle memory by
applying what you've remembered"* (l.928–929, from the music chapter — memorise the notes first, then
play until the fingers know it). That analogy is actually the right one and worth stating explicitly
in our design docs:

> **Mnemonic → recall → automaticity.** The mnemonic is scaffolding. It must be *deliberately
> retired.* A phrase still being retrieved via its story is not yet job-ready.

So our system needs a stage the book doesn't describe: **fluency reps** — the same 3 phrases, mnemonic
hidden, cued by guest audio, under a shrinking time limit, until the response is automatic. Then the
mnemonic is dropped from the card forever.

Design implication: `corpus_item` needs a per-learner state machine —
`unseen → encoded (mnemonic shown) → recalled (mnemonic on miss only) → automatic (<4s, mnemonic
retired) → maintained (spaced ladder only)`. **The CEFR level we report to HR should be driven by how
many items are `automatic`, not how many are `encoded`.** That single definition keeps us honest and
keeps the product from becoming a mnemonic toy.

Get the automaticity mechanics from Fluent Forever / Accelerated Learning / Design for How People
Learn during synthesis. This book gets us to the door.
