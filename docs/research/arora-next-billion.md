# Payal Arora — *The Next Billion Users* and the leisure critique
## Field research notes for Inglés Hotelero · 2026-07-26

**Purpose.** Not a literature review. Every section ends in "so what do we build/stop building."
The question this research answers: **what Western assumptions are baked into our motivation and
social design, and which of them are wrong for a bellboy in Cancún?**

## 1. The core thesis, in one paragraph

Development-sector and Silicon Valley thinking assumes poor users go online for utility —
jobs, health, education, crop prices. Arora's fieldwork says that is empirically false and
ideologically loaded. Poor users adopt and *sustain* digital technology overwhelmingly for
**leisure, sociality, status, romance and pleasure** — the same reasons rich Western users do.
Utility follows leisure; it does not precede it. Treating the poor as "virtuous utilitarians"
is, she argues, a colonial-era exoticism dressed as social concern.

> Her 2012 abstract, verbatim: farmers in rural India "are watched closely to see how they access
> crop prices online, while their Orkuting gets sidelined as anecdotal."

## 2. Finding-by-finding

### F1 — Leisure, not utility, drives adoption **and** retention
**Evidence (verified, 2012 paper):**
- HP's Kuppam "i-community" (Andhra Pradesh, 2002): a converted bus with laptops, printers,
  scanners, cameras. HP charged for soil testing, ration-card requests, crop prices, health info,
  video games and digital photos. **The most popular paid service was video games, then digital
  photography.** The single most popular feature overall was the free evening movie screening.
  Villagers didn't know the project's name — they knew "the mobile van."
- Almora, Central Himalayas, 8-month fieldwork, ~100 young people interviewed (2009): cybercafés
  "owed their survival to cyberleisure," mainly Orkut, Bollywood song portals, Google Images and
  Photoshopping themselves with film stars. Youth perceived computers as **leisure** devices —
  more so than phones or TV — despite media framing computers as tools of economic mobility.
- Cited corroboration: one-third of India's cable TV installations were in rural areas
  (Cooper-Chen 2005). Arora explicitly says this **breaks Maslow's hierarchy** — entertainment
  spending oversteps unmet physiological needs.
- 2015 slum ethnography: a Mumbai slum mobile store made more money downloading A/V content than
  selling SIM cards; a shared internet centre in small-town Ecuador sustained itself transferring
  ringtones.

**Assumption it kills for us:** "Our learner will practice because it's a work skill and their
job requires it." That is exactly the utilitarian premise Arora shows fails to sustain use.
Employer mandate buys us *first open*. It does not buy day 30.

**So what for IH:**
- Design the daily loop so it would be worth opening **even if nobody made you**. If the only
  reason to open it is "HR assigned it," we are building the crop-price kiosk.
- Concretely: content should be *situations with human stakes* (an angry guest at 2 a.m., a
  honeymoon upgrade, a lost passport, a drunk spring-breaker, a wedding party) — with drama,
  humour, real accents and a punchline — not "Unidad 4: Cómo dar direcciones."
- Ship at least one thing per day that has **no learning objective attached** and exists purely
  because it's enjoyable (an accent-guessing game, "¿qué dijo el huésped?", a 10-second bloopers
  clip of real guest English).

### F2 — The "virtuous poor" is a colonial fiction; morality-of-poverty framing distorts product
**Evidence (verified, 2012 + 2013):** Arora's charge is that ICT4D research is "rooted in
postcolonial discourse," and that funders steer toward measurable socio-economic outcomes because
leisure use would deter donors. She and Rangaswamy call for sidelining "morality and virtuosity of
the poor for pluralistic possibilities," and for viewing global-South users as **typical users,
not virtuous beings awaiting socio-economic liberation.** The JSMS review records her arguing the
poor are "commodified in the name of inclusivity" (p.71) and that "the poor are not radically
different from everyone else" (p.123).

**So what for IH:**
- Our design system already says **"Respeto, no condescendencia."** Arora is the academic backing
  for that instinct — treat it as validated, and enforce it harder.
- Audit all Spanish copy for the deficit register. Kill anything that reads as remedial
  ("mejora tu inglés deficiente", "nivel básico", "aún no dominas"). Replace with capability
  framing ("el inglés que ya usas + lo que sigue", "A2 → B1").
- Never justify the product to the learner on employer-benefit grounds ("para que el hotel tenga
  mejores reseñas"). Justify it on **their** grounds: propinas, ascenso, moverse a front desk,
  cruceros, otra ciudad.

### F3 — Aspiration and social status are the engine (the "want for fabulousness")
**Evidence (verified, 2015 slum ethnography, n=23, Hyderabad + Chennai, Mar–Nov 2012;
17–21 y/o, family income US$140–170/month, spending US$1–3/month on mobile internet):**
- The through-line of all their Facebook activity was "the urge to seek aspirationally endowing
  interactions with people from an elevated social status" — film stars, athletes, fitness
  figures, diaspora, educated women of higher class.
- Friending was strategic and aspirational; sending a request carried the anxiety of "making the
  first move" offline.
- Kulbeer (16, Sikh, father a truck driver) described profile-building as "cultivating a garden" —
  months of curating Sikh diaspora imagery, history and physical strength. Identity work as labour.
- Arora frames this via Appadurai's **"capacity to aspire"** — aspiration as a cultural capacity
  that development should strengthen, not moralize away.
- *(Book-level, secondhand)*: Riyadh women organizing YouTube fashion shows; young Indians adding
  "foreign" Facebook friends.
- Her podcast framing (Design Up / Use Case): "want for fabulousness by the NBU, digital products
  as an equaliser between the rich and poor."

**So what for IH:**
- **The learner's real payoff is being seen as the person who can handle the foreign guest.**
  Build for that, not for XP.
- Ship **outward-facing, learner-controlled artifacts**: a beautiful shareable card ("Nivel B1 —
  Recepción · Inglés Hotelero") sized for WhatsApp Status and Facebook, downloadable as an image,
  with their name on it. That is the "fabulousness" object. It costs us almost nothing and it is
  the highest-leverage social feature we can build.
- The certificate should be portable — it works for their *next* job too. Say so. That is
  aspiration, and it's also honest.
- Anchor levels to job identity, not abstract CEFR: "B1 = puedes manejar una queja tú solo."
  CEFR is for the HR buyer; job capability is for the learner.

### F4 — Privacy is **selective visibility**, not indifference — and it is a premium good
**Evidence (verified, 2019 *Decolonizing Privacy Studies*):**
- Arora's core move: privacy studies over-samples "Western-based, white, and middle-class"
  subjects and treats privacy as a rational market trade-off. She rejects the "privacy paradox"
  framing and argues for **"selective visibility"** — the interweaving of the need to be seen
  with the need to be hidden, "without resorting to a binary approach."
- She notes "the cost of privacy is deeply subjective and seemingly irrational as desire and
  aspiration overtakes pragmatism," and that "privacy is often not a choice."
- **Verified, 2015 slum ethnography:** "Privacy comes with a premium and public spaces are those
  that ironically afford private moments." Digital leisure is enacted at street corners, shop
  fronts and cybercafés — i.e. **in public, among peers.**
- *(Book-level, secondhand)*: Brazilians avoid geo-tagging on social media; the book asks why
  citizens under heavy state surveillance appear unconcerned about digital privacy — her answer
  in TechCrunch is that it's **not** that they don't care, it's that online space feels *more*
  private than their constrained offline lives.

**This is the finding that should scare us most.** In Arora's cases the surveilling party is the
state or the market. In ours, **it is the boss.** The HR dashboard is the surveillance apparatus.
Our current build surfaces per-employee speaking transcripts and recommendations to HR.

**So what for IH — this is a product-defining decision:**
- **Split the data contract in two.**
  - **HR sees:** CEFR level, level change over time, drills completed, cohort aggregates,
    readiness by role. Enough to justify $150–500/month.
  - **HR does not see by default:** raw audio, verbatim transcripts, individual wrong answers,
    per-question failure detail.
- Give the learner an explicit, plainly worded, Spanish-language control: *"Tu jefe ve tu nivel y
  tu progreso. No escucha tus grabaciones."* Put it in onboarding, in the recorder screen, and in
  settings. If we can't say that truthfully, change the product until we can.
- Allow **opt-in sharing** of a specific recording ("mandar esta a mi supervisor") — that is
  selective visibility, and it converts a surveillance feature into an aspiration feature.
- Do not require a real full name or photo. Allow a display name.
- Never geo-tag, never location-gate practice.

### F5 — The device and the moment are **social and semi-public**, not private and personal
**Evidence:** *(book-level, secondhand)* Himalayan teens grow closer by sharing a single computer
with common passwords and profiles. **(verified, 2015)** slum homes are 100–200 sq ft with no
private rooms, doors kept open, constant flow of visitors; informal public space is gender-
segregated; young women "do not hang out" but interact purposively. **(verified, 2010 HiWEL)** the
same small group of boys dominated shared kiosks; girls accessed them less.

**So what for IH:**
- **Assume the phone is shared and the practice moment is overheard.** A camarista practising in
  the staff break room with three coworkers present is our modal session, not the exception.
- Required features:
  1. **Modo discreto** — every speaking drill must have a text/tap fallback so a session can be
     completed without speaking aloud. Never dead-end on "no puedes continuar sin grabar."
     (We already fixed a mic-denied dead end; this is the same principle generalized.)
  2. **Captions always on, audio never loud by default**, headphone-friendly, single-tap replay.
  3. **Fast re-auth** — PIN or magic link, not a password form. Shared/borrowed device means
     frequent identity switches. Never assume one device = one person forever.
  4. **Quick-hide / low-shame surfaces** — no big red "INCORRECTO" that a coworker reads over the
     shoulder. Correction should be quiet and private.
- **Turn the constraint into a feature:** ship a *"practicar en pareja"* mode — one phone, two
  people, alternating guest/staff roles. Arora's evidence says sharing is a bonding practice, not
  a workaround to suppress. This is also the cheapest possible fix for the "no me atrevo solo"
  problem, and it makes the pre-shift briefing ritual (see F7) actually work.
- Gender asymmetry is real in her data. For a workforce with many women (camaristas, recepción),
  the private/quiet path matters more, not less.

### F6 — Play is the on-ramp to literacy. English acquisition happened *as a by-product of romance.*
**Evidence (verified, 2015):** Slum youth, who joined Facebook to friend girls and celebrities,
**"reported learning English, typing with a keyboard, developing sociable personalities and
acquiring general knowledge via Facebook mediated interactions."** A 20-year-old described the
transformation: before Facebook he could not speak to girls at all; after a year of chatting,
"I can look at them and speak with them." He explains why: **"On FB, one can talk freely without
having any fear."** The authors' conclusion: leisure engagements are "a powerful precursor to
developing technology literacies, skills and ecologies of learning," and the separation between
leisure use and developmental use is "artificial... and by and large, a facile one."

Arora & Rangaswamy (2013), verbatim: **"ICTs are social artifacts before they become tools for
development."** Radio in 1930s India was pitched as a weapon against illiteracy; it entered
everyday life only once it served entertainment.

**This is the single most transferable finding to a speaking product.**

**So what for IH:**
- **The core product value is not the curriculum. It is the low-stakes rehearsal space.**
  "Aquí puedes hablar mal inglés sin que nadie se entere." That sentence is arguably our
  positioning line, our onboarding line, and our objection-handler for an embarrassed 45-year-old
  bellboy — and it is *empirically grounded*, not a marketing conceit.
- Therefore: recordings are **private by default, re-recordable without limit, and deletable.**
  We currently allow one regrab on speaking prompts. Under this finding, that cap is a mistake for
  practice mode (keep the cap only for the *placement exam*, where it's an assessment integrity
  requirement). Unlimited retries in practice; one take in the exam.
- Build the drill as **conversation, not exercise.** A chat-shaped roleplay with a guest character
  who reacts, where the reward is the guest's reaction (smile, tip, thanks, escalation avoided) —
  not a score. WhatsApp is already the native surface for this; lean into it.
- Give guest characters personality and continuity across days. Recurring characters are the
  cheapest retention mechanic available and they cost content, not engineering.

### F7 — Self-serve learning tech **decays** without a human mediator. This is documented failure.
**Evidence (verified, 2010 "Hope-in-the-Wall?"):** Arora went to find the Hole-in-the-Wall kiosks
in Almora and Hawalbagh (Central Himalayas). She found them **abandoned and in ruins.** Teachers
showed "a general lack of curiosity over, or concern for, the fate of the kiosk"; they recalled
"a few boys using these kiosks, but usually for things like games, that's all." The caretaker had
been employed by HiWEL, was **not paid** to maintain the equipment, and had not heard from the
organisation in months. She also notes HiWEL's own data showed fewer girls than boys accessing
kiosks, and her earlier HP fieldwork found kiosks nicknamed *"play stations"*, dominated by the
same group of boys. Her conclusion: **"Autonomous learning with computers without some monitoring
and mediators may continue to face resistance"** — on grounds of both order and equity.

Note the nuance: she is *not* anti-play. She is saying **play without a mediator does not become
skill, and unmaintained self-serve infrastructure dies.**

**So what for IH — this is the highest-risk gap in our current plan:**
- A PWA + WhatsApp drip with an HR dashboard is structurally the Hole-in-the-Wall. It **will**
  decay in a property unless a named human inside the hotel owns the ritual.
- **Ship a "Campeón/Coordinador de práctica" role as a first-class product object,** not a
  services afterthought:
  - It is a *named person* (usually a shift supervisor or head bellboy), visible in the app.
  - Their weekly job is **≤5 minutes**: one screen showing "quién no ha practicado esta semana"
    plus one pre-written WhatsApp nudge they can send with one tap.
  - Give them status: a badge, their name on the property's progress page, mention in the monthly
    HR report. Status is the payment (F3 applies to the mediator too).
- **Sell a ritual, not software.** The pilot offer should be "5 minutos en el briefing de turno,
  tres veces por semana" with a printed card for the supervisor. Note the tension with the HR
  buyer's "near-zero implementation work" demand — resolve it by making the mediator's job
  genuinely tiny and scripted, not by removing the mediator.
- **Instrument decay explicitly.** Alert when a property's weekly active rate falls below a
  threshold, and alert the *account owner*, not just the dashboard. Kiosks die quietly.

### F8 — Frugal innovation / *jugaad*: users will hack around our constraints. Design for the hack.
**Evidence:** *(book-level, secondhand — ch. 3 "Media Bandits", ch. 4 "The Virtuous Poor")*
frugal innovation and grassroots piracy economies; the JSMS review records the poor "left to build
their own creative systems through 'frugal innovation'" (p.72). **(verified, 2015)** users ran
Nimbuzz on top of the Facebook API to cut voice/chat costs on short-term unlimited data plans;
they bought "deal of the day" micro-plans (₹5/day ≈ US$0.04) discovered by comparing offers at
neighbourhood mobile shops; they consumed Facebook "byte by byte" on feature phones, with
"enormous dexterity" needed to assemble a coherent experience from fragments.

**So what for IH:**
- Our learners are on prepaid plans, spotty data, mid-range Android, limited storage. They **will**:
  share an account, do a week's drills in one sitting on hotel WiFi, screenshot answers, practise
  in pairs, and use a coworker's phone. **Do not fight any of this.**
- Concrete rules:
  - **No hard daily gates.** Content must be batchable — let them do 7 days on their day off.
  - **Offline-first with a durable answer queue.** (Already on the post-MVP list — Arora's evidence
    promotes it from "hardening" to "core requirement.")
  - **Aggressive asset budget.** Pre-generated compressed audio, no autoplay video on cellular,
    an explicit "descargar la semana en WiFi" button.
  - **Never show an upload-failure state to the learner.** (Already our contract — keep it.)
  - Storage-frugal: PWA install must be small; purge cached audio after use.

### F9 — Optimism and hope are the dominant register outside the West
**Evidence:** *(From Pessimism to Promise, 2024; re:publica 2024 talk writeup; MIT Press
description — book not read in full)* Arora argues Western discourse suffers "pessimism paralysis"
toward AI, while Global South youth show "a contagion of optimism"; tech affords young people
"a rare space for self-actualization"; she calls for **"algorithms of aspiration"** and a shift to
**relationally-driven design.** Her line: pessimism is a privilege for those who can afford
despair. Keynote framing: ~90% of the world's youth live outside the West.

**So what for IH:**
- **Register:** aspirational and forward-leaning, never cautionary or remedial. This is a tone
  brief, and it constrains copy, illustration and photography.
- **"Relationally-driven design"** operationalized: progress framed relative to *people and roles*
  ("ya manejas el check-in como recepción"), not to abstract percentages.
- **Watch the honesty line.** Optimism ≠ over-promising. Don't imply an English certificate
  guarantees a promotion. Aspiration in *register*, precision in *claims*.

### F10 — Don't run a poverty laboratory
**Evidence:** *(book-level, secondhand — ch. 6 "The Poverty Laboratory")* the JSMS review records
Arora confronting "powerful industries that exploit the poor, and experiment with their learning
through investments," and the line that **"the poor have everything to lose"** (p.123) and need
*less* innovation, not more. She critiques Negroponte's One Laptop Per Child and Mitra's
Hole-in-the-Wall by name. Also: data from poor users is collected and reused to surveil the
community, while insights from that data stay inaccessible to them.

**So what for IH:**
- **Publish a one-page learner data promise in Spanish, inside the app.** What HR sees, what HR
  never sees, how long recordings are kept, how to delete them. This is a trust asset and a
  competitive differentiator against generic corporate LMS.
- **Never let scores be used for termination without the learner knowing the rule.** Put it in the
  HR contract language: this is a training and placement instrument, not a disciplinary one.
  Say it to the buyer *and* to the learner. If a buyer insists otherwise, that shapes the deal —
  the learner-side resistance it creates will kill adoption anyway.
- **Give data back to the learner.** Their own progress view should be richer than HR's, not
  poorer. Arora's specific complaint is that the community generates the data and never sees it.
- Don't A/B test anything that can affect a learner's standing at work. Test copy, order, format —
  not score thresholds.

## 3. Format grounding (not Arora — independent, verified)

From the **Ericsson Mobility Report, November 2025** (full PDF read, figures verbatim):
- "At the end of 2025, video traffic is expected to account for **76 percent** of all mobile data
  traffic."
- "Social media-generated videos constitute **70–80 percent** of all mobile video traffic viewed on
  smartphones, while streaming of video-on-demand services represents **less than 10 percent**,
  underscoring users' preference for short, engaging clips from social platforms."
  *(Sampled across four European networks — treat the exact split as indicative, not LATAM-specific.)*
- Data traffic per smartphone, 2025: **Latin America 15 GB/month** (North America 25; India 36;
  Sub-Saharan Africa 5.3). Forecast LATAM 31 GB/month by 2031.

**So what:** the native media grammar our learner already lives in is **short, vertical, captioned,
social-feed video** — not long-form courseware and not VOD. Our drills should look like the feed.
And 15 GB/month across a whole prepaid household is not a lot: our per-session data budget is a
real design constraint, not a nicety.

## 4. Where Arora does **not** transfer cleanly (tensions to hold)

1. **She studies voluntary consumer adoption; we are employer-sponsored.**
   The mandate buys us first-open — the thing leisure buys in her cases. So her frame bites hardest
   not on *acquisition* but on **day-7 to day-90 retention.** Don't use "but it's mandatory" as a
   reason to skip the pleasure work; that's exactly the trap.
2. **In her work the surveiller is the state or the market. In ours it's the boss.**
   Proximity makes it worse, not better. A distant state can't cut your shifts. This *raises* the
   stakes on the F4 data contract.
3. **Her leisure subjects skew young, male, with discretionary time** (n=23, all but two male,
   17–21). A 42-year-old camarista on a double shift with kids is a different person. Do not
   over-index on "make it fun" to the point of making it *longer*. **Five minutes must stay five
   minutes.** Pleasure per minute, not more minutes.
4. **The buyer genuinely needs measurement.** Arora's critique of utilitarian framing applies to
   the *learner-facing* surface. The HR surface should stay ruthlessly utilitarian and
   quantitative — that's what $150–500/month buys. **Two audiences, two value propositions, one
   dataset, different projections.** That split is the main architectural takeaway of this whole
   research pass.
5. **Her critique of self-directed learning cuts both ways.** She is skeptical of leaving people
   alone with a screen (F7) *and* skeptical of school-like structure (she notes HiWEL ended up
   using school benchmarks — English proficiency among them — while claiming to escape schooling).
   The resolution is a **mediated but non-scholastic** loop: human ritual + playful content.

## 6. Design rules (hand these to a designer as-is)

**DO**
1. Make the app the safe place to be bad at English: private by default, unlimited retries, quiet corrections.
2. State the privacy contract in plain Spanish, in-product: *"Tu jefe ve tu nivel. No escucha tus grabaciones."*
3. Give the learner a beautiful, shareable, name-on-it level card sized for WhatsApp Status.
4. Write content as situations with human stakes and recurring guest characters, not as units.
5. Ship a text/tap fallback for every speaking drill (*modo discreto*) — a session must never dead-end on the mic.
6. Design for a shared/borrowed phone: PIN or magic-link re-auth, fast identity switch, no shoulder-surfable red failure states.
7. Ship a one-phone two-person "practicar en pareja" mode.
8. Make everything batchable and offline-first; add an explicit "descargar la semana en WiFi" control.
9. Name a human Campeón per property with a ≤5-minute scripted weekly ritual, and give them visible status.
10. Frame levels as job capability first ("ya manejas una queja sola"), CEFR second.
11. Keep pleasure-per-minute high and total minutes at five.
12. Give the learner a richer view of their own data than HR gets.

**DON'T**
1. Don't show HR raw audio or verbatim transcripts by default.
2. Don't rank named employees against each other on any screen visible inside the property.
3. Don't use punitive streaks, streak-loss animations, or daily lockouts.
4. Don't use deficit/remedial copy, or justify the product to the learner on the hotel's behalf.
5. Don't require real full name or profile photo; don't geo-tag or location-gate.
6. Don't assume one device = one person, or that the learner is alone and quiet.
7. Don't dead-end on denied mic, no data, or failed upload.
8. Don't ship a purely self-serve property rollout — unmediated kiosks decay.
9. Don't A/B test anything that can affect a learner's standing at work.
10. Don't confuse the HR value prop (measurement) with the learner value prop (status + safety + pleasure).

## 7. What to validate in the pilot (Arora's frame turned into tests)

1. **Leisure hypothesis.** Ship two drill styles to matched groups: "situación con personaje" vs.
   "ejercicio funcional." Measure day-30 voluntary opens (opens *not* preceded by a WhatsApp nudge).
   Prediction: situations win on voluntary opens, tie on accuracy.
2. **Privacy hypothesis.** Ask learners directly, pre-launch: *"¿te sentirías cómodo si tu jefe
   escuchara tus grabaciones?"* Then measure speaking-drill completion in a property told
   "HR escucha" vs. one told "HR no escucha." Prediction: large gap. This is a cheap, decisive test.
3. **Mediator hypothesis.** One property with a named Campeón + briefing ritual, one without.
   Measure week-8 weekly-active rate. Prediction: unmediated property is <40% of mediated.
4. **Status artifact hypothesis.** Instrument the share-card. Measure share rate and whether
   sharers retain better. Cheap to build, high information value.
5. **Shared-device reality check.** Just ask, in the placement-exam intake: *"¿este teléfono es
   sólo tuyo?"* and *"¿dónde vas a practicar?"* We are currently guessing. One question ends the guessing.
6. **Discreet-mode demand.** Log how often learners choose the text fallback over the mic. If it's
   >25%, embarrassment/setting is a bigger constraint than we modelled and the roadmap changes.
